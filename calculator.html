The open question on the summary page is what kind of income is the contract profit. The IRS has not said. So this page does not pick one. It takes the same dollar amount, in $10,000 steps from $10,000 to $200,000, and computes the modeled federal and California tax under every candidate character at once. Move the slider and all five move together, so the difference is the answer you can actually read.

Read the model before reading the number

The selected amount is treated as total taxable income, not gross Kalshi deposits, contract notional, account balance, or necessarily the P&L shown by Kalshi. Federal uses the tax-year 2026 single-filer tables in Revenue Procedure 2025-32. California uses FTB’s 2025 Schedule X (single or married/RDP filing separately) in every scenario, because FTB says California has no lower rate for capital gains.

Included

  • Five candidate characters: ordinary, short-term capital, section 1256 (60/40), long-term capital, wagering.
  • Federal ordinary brackets for tax year 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32 § 4.01, Table 3).
  • Federal 0 / 15 / 20 percent stacking under § 1(h) with the 2026 breakpoints $49,450 and $545,500 (§ 1(j)(5); Rev. Proc. 2025-32 § 4.03).
  • California Schedule X, 2025, applied identically to every scenario.
  • Progressive rates: the next step can cross a bracket.

Not included

  • Deductions, credits, dependents, or other income.
  • NIIT, AMT, self-employment tax, penalties, or withholding.
  • Loss arithmetic. Loss rules differ sharply by character and are described below, not computed.
  • Year-end mark-to-market timing under § 1256, and whether California follows it.
  • A conclusion about the character of any particular Kalshi contract.

Why the California year matters: the research record does not contain a final 2026 California individual rate schedule (a request for 2026-540-tax-rate-schedules.pdf returned FTB’s 404 page on 24 September 2026). The 2026 FTB Form 540-ES instructions tell filers to use the 2025 tax table in that estimated-tax worksheet. This page therefore labels the California source as 2025 instead of silently calling it 2026. FTB also says the rate schedule is for taxable income over $100,000 and the Tax Table applies at or below; the schedule formula is used at every step here, so rows at or below $100,000 can differ from the Tax Table by rounding (F49).

How to read the comparison

Three rows tie on rate, not on losses

Ordinary, short-term capital, and wagering produce the same federal dollars in this model because all three are taxed on the ordinary table and the amount is already net taxable income. Where they differ is what happens to losses and which form is used. That is why the wagering row is not “free”: with any losses, its 90 percent federal cap and itemizing requirement make the real burden higher than the number shown.

Two rows are lower only if the character holds

Section 1256 and long-term capital are lower because 60 percent or 100 percent of the amount rides the 0 / 15 / 20 percent stack. Neither is established for these contracts. Section 1256 requires the contract to be a section 1256 contract, and 60/40 never applies to gain that is otherwise ordinary (§ 1256(f)(2)). Long-term requires an actual holding period over one year.

California does not move

The California column is identical in every scenario. FTB: “California does not have a lower rate for capital gains. All capital gains are taxed as ordinary income.” The character question changes the federal number only. It still changes California loss treatment: California does not conform to the federal 90 percent wagering cap.

Worked example at $200,000, so the arithmetic can be checked by hand

Ordinary / short-term / wagering

Table 3: over $105,700 but not over $201,775 → $17,966 plus 24% of the excess over $105,700.
$17,966 + 0.24 × ($200,000 − $105,700) = $17,966 + $22,632 = $40,598.00.

Section 1256 (60/40)

Ordinary slice 40% = $80,000: $5,800 + 0.22 × ($80,000 − $50,400) = $12,312.00.
Long-term slice 60% = $120,000. Zero-rate room = $49,450 − $80,000 < 0, so none at 0%. All $120,000 sits below $545,500 → 15% = $18,000.00.
Federal = $12,312 + $18,000 = $30,312.00, which is $10,286.00 less than ordinary.

Long-term capital gain

Ordinary slice = $0. Zero-rate room = $49,450 → 0% on $49,450. Remaining $150,550 at 15% = $22,582.50.

California, every scenario

Schedule X: over $72,724 but not over $371,479 → $3,201.97 plus 9.30% of the amount over $72,724.
$3,201.97 + 0.093 × ($200,000 − $72,724) = $3,201.97 + $11,836.67 = $15,038.64.

Source trail and limits

Federal rate table and breakpoints

Revenue Procedure 2025-32, section 4.01 Table 3 (ordinary) and section 4.03 (maximum zero-rate and 15 percent amounts).

Stacking rule

26 U.S.C. § 1(h)(1)(A)–(D), as modified by § 1(j)(5). Ordinary income is counted first; adjusted net capital gain is stacked on top at 0, 15, then 20 percent.

Read 26 U.S.C. § 1

60/40 and its limits

26 U.S.C. § 1256(a)(3) gives the split; § 1256(b)(2)(B), (f)(2), and (g)(1) are the limits quoted in the ledger.
