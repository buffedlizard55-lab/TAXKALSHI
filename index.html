For a U.S. citizen who lives in California and pays tax in the ordinary way, Kalshi earnings are taxable. The IRS has not published a ruling that says what kind of income the event-contract profit is. California taxes a resident’s taxable income at ordinary rates and does not have a lower capital-gains rate.

Taxable — settled Character — open California rate — settled 60/40 claim — not established

Executive answer

The person in scope is a U.S. citizen, a California resident, and an ordinary filer. The facts exclude withholding and penalties: no backup withholding, no unpaid-tax posture, and no penalty computation. The income in scope is Kalshi earnings — event-contract profit or loss, plus interest and credits the platform itself separates from that profit.

  1. The profit is income. Internal Revenue Code section 61 taxes income from whatever source derived unless another section excludes it. No exclusion for this profit was located. 26 U.S.C. § 61.
  2. No form does not mean no tax. Kalshi’s help center lists Form 1099-INT for interest, Form 1099-MISC for credits and rewards, Form 1099-B for crypto-transfer broker proceeds, and Form 1099-DA for ZeroHash digital-asset reporting. It does not list a form for event-contract profit. The same page says the profit-and-loss statement is not tax advice. Kalshi tax documentation, 21 June 2026.
  3. Federal character is not settled. Four candidate treatments are in the Code. None has an IRS ruling that names these contracts. Short-term capital gain and ordinary income can share a federal rate. They do not share loss rules. Section 1256’s 60/40 split is a consequence, not a label that follows from CFTC registration.
  4. California does not follow the federal capital-gain rates. The Franchise Tax Board says: “California does not have a lower rate for capital gains. All capital gains are taxed as ordinary income.” A resident is taxed on entire taxable income. Rates run from 1 percent to 12.3 percent, plus 1 percent on taxable income over $1,000,000. FTB capital gains · RTC § 17041 · RTC § 17043.
  5. Withholding is outside the facts, and it is not described for contract trading. Kalshi’s tax article describes information returns, not withholding on event-contract profits. Information reporting is not withholding. Estimated-tax rules still exist. They are noted so “no withholding” is not read as “pay nothing until April.” Penalties are not calculated.

This page is a research record. It is not a California CPA engagement, not an IRS determination, and not advice for a particular return. The practitioner seats in the decision engine are empty until a state board lookup is recorded.

Short-term capital gain (held one year or less) Taxed as ordinary income at the 2026 graduated rates. Losses net against capital gains, then up to $3,000 against other income ($1,500 if married filing separately), with a carryforward. Form 8949 and Schedule D, if this character is used. Same dollars of gain, taxed at ordinary California rates. Holding period does not lower the California rate. Topic 409 describes this path for a capital asset. No IRS page retrieved says an event contract is a capital asset. Section 1221 was not line-read in this pass. See remaining work.
Long-term capital gain (held more than one year) Preferential 0, 15, or 20 percent rates on adjusted net capital gain, using the 2026 breakpoints in Rev. Proc. 2025-32 § 4.03. The 3.8 percent net investment income tax may apply above the Topic 559 thresholds if the gain is net investment income. Still ordinary California rates. The federal preference stops at the state return. Many contracts resolve inside a year. A long holding period has to be counted, not assumed. Topic 409: count from the day after acquisition through the day of disposition.
Section 1256 contract 60 percent long-term and 40 percent short-term regardless of holding period. Open contracts are marked to market at year-end. IRS Form 6781 is the form titled for these contracts. Its instructions were not line-read in this pass, so line numbers are not stated. No California preferential rate on the 60 percent slice. Whether California adopts the year-end mark for these contracts was not confirmed by an FTB sentence. Left open. A regulated futures contract must be traded on a qualified board or exchange and the deposit and withdrawal amounts must depend on marking to market — both elements are now quoted from § 1256(g)(1) (F45). Section 1256(f)(2) adds that 60/40 never applies to gain that is otherwise ordinary (F46). KalshiEX is a DCM. Kalshi Klear told the CFTC on 26 March 2026 that these contracts are fully collateralized swaps and “are not traded on margin.” That cuts against a casual 60/40 claim. It is not an IRS ruling.
Ordinary income that is not capital gain and not wagering Taxed at the same graduated rates as other ordinary income. Loss treatment is not the $3,000 capital-loss cap and is not the wagering cap. No IRS page retrieved tells a filer to use this path for event contracts. Ordinary California rates, if the amount is in California taxable income. This path is conservative on the federal rate for a short holding period, because short-term capital gain is already taxed at ordinary rates. It is not established. Section 1221 was not line-read, so this pass cannot say the contract fails to be a capital asset.
Wagering Winnings are reported in full. For tax years beginning after 31 December 2025, the deduction is 90 percent of wagering losses, only to the extent of wagering gains, and only if the taxpayer itemizes. Topic 419, retrieved the same day, still omits the 90 percent rule. The statute controls. FTB says California does not conform to that 90 percent limitation, or to the earlier federal clarification that other wagering expenses are inside the loss limit. Topic 419’s list “includes but isn’t limited to” lotteries, raffles, sports betting, horse races, and casinos. It does not name event contracts. Sports-outcome contracts are the closest analogy. Non-sports contracts are a weaker analogy. Neither analogy is a holding.
Do not blend these rows. A year cannot be section 1256 for the gains and wagering for the losses. The engine’s working posture is to report the profit, keep the streams separate, and not claim 60/40 or wagering treatment unless a later, sourced analysis of the particular contract supports it.

Working posture, until a ruling or a verified CPA engagement says otherwise

Do this

  • Report contract profit even if no 1099 covers it.
  • Report interest and credits on their own, as ordinary income.
  • Keep Kalshi’s P&L as a worksheet. It is not the return, and its FIFO label is not tax advice.
  • Apply California ordinary rates to whatever amount is in California taxable income.
  • If there is no withholding, read the estimated-tax rules before assuming the April payment is penalty-free. Do not compute a penalty from this site.

Do not do this on the strength of a blog

  • Do not claim the 60/40 split because Kalshi is a designated contract market.
  • Do not treat a missing 1099 as a zero.
  • Do not put the federal long-term rate on the California return.
  • Do not put the federal 90 percent wagering-loss cap on the California return. FTB says California does not conform.
  • Do not use Topic 419’s current loss paragraph for a 2026 federal wagering year. It lags section 165(d).

The critique that produced this posture is on the decision engine page. Each sentence in the ledger has a quote and a link.
